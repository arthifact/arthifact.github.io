From March to August 2026 I worked with Professor Julien Chaput at the University of Texas at El Paso on a research project in full-waveform inversion (FWI), a way to image the ground from seismic recordings. My question was whether a few simple rules could decide how the seismic model is built, instead of a fixed grid.

## Problem

FWI sends sound waves into the ground, records the echoes at the surface, and searches for the velocity map whose simulated echoes match them. From a poor starting guess it lines up the wrong wave cycles and gets stuck. The usual fix is to start at low frequencies and build a smooth model first, but that smooth model is normally a pixel grid or a fixed layout of shapes, the same everywhere whether the geology needs it or not.

## How it works

I build the smooth model out of Gaussians, soft tilted ellipses added on top of a linear background, where velocity grows steadily with depth. Each one is described by:

- **Center:** its depth and horizontal position.
- **Two widths:** one along the ellipse and one across it, so it can be long and thin or round.
- **Angle:** how much it is tilted.
- **Amplitude:** how much faster or slower the waves travel inside it.

The model starts with no Gaussians. Every tenth step is a growth round where these rules decide what to add and what to remove. In the steps in between, the Adam optimizer updates all six numbers of every Gaussian at once.

- **Where:** the gradient is split into connected patches of the same sign, after cutting weak links below 1% of each patch's peak. Each patch is one candidate. A Gauss-Newton estimate, using how well each area is illuminated by the shots, predicts how much each candidate would lower the misfit, and the best six are tried.
- **Shape:** the center is the gradient-weighted center of the patch, and the two widths and the angle come from the patch's weighted covariance. A patch that runs along a dipping layer becomes a Gaussian tilted along it.

(d) The velocity model after the round, with existing Gaussians in white and the four newly kept ones in red.">
  One growth round at 6 Hz on Marmousi: (a) the gradient, (b) its same-sign patches, (c) one Gaussian per patch, with the six that are tried in black, and (d) the four that paid their price and stayed.

- **Amplitude:** a first try at 5% of the local speed, in the downhill direction. A parabola through the measured losses then picks the amplitude, capped at 30% of the local speed.
- **Size:** no width is ever smaller than a quarter of the local wavelength, the smallest detail the data can resolve at that frequency. The floor is checked when a Gaussian is born and after every Adam step.
- **Keep or remove:** a new Gaussian stays only if it lowers the misfit by more than a price from the Bayesian information criterion: six parameters times the log of the number of effective data samples, scaled by the noise level. Gaussians that fade below 5 m/s are removed if the misfit rises by less than what they cost. Each band keeps the model with the lowest misfit plus price.

The Gaussians are fitted at 6 Hz and then 9 Hz. After that the smooth model is frozen, and ordinary pixel inversion adds detail at 13, 20 and 30 Hz.

## Results

On Marmousi, every method gets the same data and the same final pixel stages, so only the smooth model differs. The adaptive Gaussians end with the lowest error and recover the fast layer at the lower left most clearly.

I then tested the method on four standard models (Marmousi, Overthrust, BP 2004 and SEAM) against a fixed grid of Gaussians and three pixel methods. The pixel methods stayed stuck near the starting guess. The adaptive model gave the best smooth model in most cases, even when the fixed grid got the same amount of computing, with the clearest win on Overthrust. On SEAM the fixed grid did better.
