Full-waveform inversion (FWI) makes a picture of the ground from sound. We send waves down, record the echoes at the surface, and search for the velocity map whose simulated echoes match the recorded ones. From a poor starting guess it lines up the wrong wave cycles and gets stuck, so the usual practice is to start with low frequencies, build a smooth *macro model* first, and add detail later.

This project asks one question: **during that smooth stage, can a few rules from statistics, signal processing and wave physics choose how the model is described, instead of a fixed grid?** I build the smooth model out of Gaussians, smooth bumps that are each described by six numbers, and let the rules decide where each one goes, what shape it takes and whether it is worth keeping.

## The method

The inversion runs in two stages. **Stage 1** (6 and 9 Hz) builds the smooth model from Gaussians. **Stage 2** (13, 20 and 30 Hz) freezes it and refines every 10 m cell with ordinary pixel FWI. Every method I compare uses the same Stage 2, so any difference comes from Stage 1.

Stage 1 starts with no Gaussians. Nine steps out of ten, all Gaussians are fine-tuned together. Every tenth step, six rules decide what to add and what to remove:

1. **Where.** Split the gradient, which says where the velocity should go up or down, into patches that all want the same change. One Gaussian has one sign, so each patch is one candidate.
2. **Shape.** Give each candidate the centre, stretch and tilt of its patch, so a dipping layer gets a long, tilted Gaussian.
3. **Smallest size.** Never narrower than a quarter wavelength. At that width a Gaussian keeps under 1% of its strength at the finest detail the band can see, so it cannot invent detail the data cannot check.
4. **Ranking.** Predict each candidate's gain, corrected for illumination so that patches near the sources do not always win, and test the top six.
5. **Strength.** Try a small 5% step, fit a parabola, cap at 30%, and keep whichever measured better.
6. **Keep or remove.** A Gaussian stays only if it lowers the misfit by more than a fixed price $\tau$, and fading ones are removed at the same price.

The price comes from the Bayesian information criterion. With noise variance $\sigma^2$, $n_{\text{eff}}$ independent data samples and six numbers per Gaussian,

$$
\tau \;\propto\; 6\,\sigma^{2}\ln n_{\text{eff}} .
$$

It has no tuning knob, it is zero for clean data, and in practice growth stops once the misfit reaches the noise level.

## Results

I tested four standard synthetic models: Marmousi, Overthrust, BP 2004 and SEAM. Each is a 700 m × 700 m section with five surface sources and noise added to the data, and each run is repeated with three noise patterns. Every method starts from the same guess. The comparisons are plain pixels, smoothed pixels, total-variation pixels and a **fixed grid of Gaussians**, which uses the same bumps on a regular lattice with only their strengths fitted.

<Figure id="fig-errors" number={3} src={errors} alt="Two grouped bar charts of velocity error for the four models, after the smooth stage and after the final stage. Bars show the best pixel method, the fixed grid and the adaptive Gaussians; a black line marks the starting error.">
  Velocity error, averaged over three noise patterns. Lower is better; the black line is the starting guess.
</Figure>

- **Pixels stay stuck.** From this start, all three pixel methods end near their starting error. Smoothing and total variation do not fix that.
- **Adaptive beats fixed after the smooth stage.** It gives the most similar picture to the truth in all 12 cases and the lowest error in 9.
- **Not just extra computing.** With the same amount of computing, the fixed grid still loses in 10 of 12 cases.
- **Where it loses.** After the final stage the fixed grid has the lower error in 5 of 12 runs, including all three on SEAM, where the adaptive model finds the salt's shape but underestimates the deep velocity.

## What it shows

Letting justified rules choose the building blocks of the smooth model works: it gives a better macro model than a fixed layout in most cases, even at equal computing, and pixel methods cannot match it from a poor start. It is not better everywhere, it costs more simulations in Stage 1, and these tests are small and two-dimensional. Next steps are larger and 3-D models, more sources, field data, and the same rules with building blocks other than Gaussians.
