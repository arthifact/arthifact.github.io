## Problem

Game engines like Unreal Engine swap an object for simpler versions as it moves away from the camera. This is not about close-ups: up close the original mesh is what you want. Far away, its small triangles end up smaller than a pixel and stop adding anything.

The usual far versions are a lower-resolution mesh or a billboard, a flat picture that faces the camera. A low-resolution mesh loses thin parts like ears first, and a billboard has no depth, so it breaks when the view or the light changes. Voxels keep the volume: they still block light, cast and receive shadows, hide what is behind them and move with the object. So, taking the DMesh++ pipeline as given, the goal was a level of detail that hands the far parts of a mesh over to voxels, block by block, with a rule that gradients can follow instead of hand-tuned switches.

## How it works

DMesh++ gives every candidate triangle a probability instead of an on/off value, so the mesh connectivity can change smoothly during optimization. On top of that:

- **Distance:** a voxel covers about four pixels on screen, so the farther the object, the bigger a voxel can be.
- **Mesh or voxel:** split the Stanford bunny into blocks, and switch a block to voxels once its triangles are smaller than a voxel. Close up nothing changes; far away the whole bunny is voxels.
- **Pixel to voxel:** match one column of voxels to each pixel of a silhouette, and fit the voxels by gradient descent.
- **Silhouettes:** compare the outline of each version against the full scan from eight angles.
